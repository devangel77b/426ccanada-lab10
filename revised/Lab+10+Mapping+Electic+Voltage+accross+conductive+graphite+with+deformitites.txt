Mapping Electric Voltage Across Conductive Graphite with Deformities


Akula, T.; Arenas, M.; Canada, C.; Jajoo, D.; Lavrenov, A.; Neyir, T.
January 30, 2026
Abstract: 


Non-destructive testing (NDT) methods are critical for evaluating the reliability of electrical components without damaging them, starting with the testing of the components’ materials and metallic surfaces. In this experiment, an electric current flowed through a conductive medium, and electric potential was measured. Defects were mimicked on the medium, and electric potential was measured again and compared. This study demonstrates how electrical measurements can reveal flaws without damaging the material.


Introduction: 


3D printing conductive materials, like metal, poses problems such as cracks, empty spaces, and non-uniform conductivity. To develop a non-destructive testing (NDT) technique to detect these flaws, we supplied a known current into a conductive object and measured the electric potential at certain locations, and again with gaps in the conductive material to measure the differences. This approach allows defects to be identified without damaging the material, making it suitable for non-destructive evaluation, and relies on the fact that electric potential changes predictably in an intact conductor.


In this experiment, graphite on paper simulates the printed conductive material, and current was applied using a 9-Volt battery connected to the graphite surface using paper clips. By erasing areas on the graphite surface, we simulated material defects and measured the resulting electric potentials. In a uniform conductive region, electric potential is expected to change gradually due to resistance along the material, while any sudden change in measured voltage indicates a disruption in current flow caused by a defect or discontinuity.


We hypothesize that the uniform block of graphite will have an approximately linear scale between distance and voltage. For the non-uniform block of graphite with a gap in the center, we hypothesize that the electric potential will decrease by a larger scale [a][b][c]than the uniform block after the gap due to the interruption of current flow and change in surface area.


Background:
A battery provides an electric potential difference, which establishes an electric field within a circuit. This electric field drives an electric current through a wire, whose resistance causes energy to be dissipated along the path. When an electric potential is applied to a continuous wire, the electric potential changes gradually along the length of the wire due to its resistance. Given by equations (1) & (2):
(1) V=IR                (2) R=⍴L/A
However, when the electric potential is interrupted by a discontinuity in the wire, or any electrical component, charge can no longer move freely through that region. This will create disruptions in a circuit, leading to unwanted failures. Instead, charge accumulates at the edges of the discontinuity, generating a strong electric field in the surrounding area. As a result, the current distribution near the discontinuity becomes uneven, with the current becoming more prominent near the edges. A significant localized voltage drop is also produced at this point due to the interruption in the wire. Therefore, by measuring the electric potential at different points along the wire, discontinuities can be identified by observing sudden and abnormal changes in electric potential. This is important to ensure that circuit components are not faulty, and circuits work as intended.
Even though these principles are often considered for metal wires they could also be implemented for a conductive surface like the graphite we are using in our trials. In our experiment, the graphite is what acts as our flat conductor, and any breaks can also be detected by changes in voltage.[d][e]


Experimental Design: 


The conductive medium consisted of graphite deposited on paper using a No. 2 pencil, forming a rectangular conductive region 5 centimeters long and 1 centimeter wide. uniform graphite distribution was ensured by pressing down hard with the pencil so current could flow uniformly across the entire surface. Electrical contact was made at opposite ends of the graphite using paper clips connected to a 9-volt battery.  This supplied a constant potential difference across the graphite surface. During voltage measurements, the negative probe of the multimeter was fixed to the negative terminal of the battery and defined as 0-volt (ground), while the positive probe was moved along the graphite starting at the 5 cm position and then at measured intervals.


A digital multimeter was used to measure voltage and resistance. During voltage measurements, the negative probe was fixed to the negative electrode, serving as a reference ground, while the positive probe was placed at various locations along the conductive region.


Two configurations were tested: a uniform 5 centimeters by 1 centimeter graphite region with no defects, and a 5 centimeters by 1 centimeter graphite region containing a 1 centimeter by 0.5 centimeter centered, therefore making two 0.25 centimeter bridges on either side. Voltage measurements were repeated to observe how the defect affected the electric potential across the conductor.


This process was only done once; no repeated trials.


Procedure: 


First, graphite was applied evenly to the paper by repeatedly drawing in a 5-centimeter by 1-centimeter box to form a conductive region at the edge of the paper. Paperclips were slid on that edge, touching each end of the box, allowing for electrodes to attach to the ends. The 9-volt battery was connected to establish a steady current flow, with the negative terminal defined as 0 V (ground). Voltage measurements were taken by moving the positive electrode at known distances from the reference electrode from right to left: 5, 4, 3, 2, and 1 centimeter(s).[f] The total resistance of the graphite region was measured. In the center of the box, a gap of 1 centimeter by 0.5 centimeter was introduced, and the voltage measurements were repeated with an additional metric of 2.5 centimeters in the middle of the gap.

Given the found voltage in our tests, we concluded that the uniform block of graphite matched our hypothesis through its linear pattern of relation between voltage and distance. We have failed to reject both of the null hypotheses, as our results have sufficiently described our hypothesis that we predicted that the graph would be linear with no deformities and fall off with deformities.  


Discussion:


A larger surface area for the graphite would have provided both advantages. A larger, wider conductor has a greater cross-sectional area, attributing a lower resistance. However, depending on how well the conductor is made, a larger amount of imperfection is also likely due to the greater amount of error possible on a larger surface, further decreasing the resistance than within a smaller, more controlled area. Additionally, there are also discrepancies involving the paper clips supplying the graphite surface with current: only the tips of the paper clips were in contact with the graphite; had they been in more contact, the electric potential would be easily measured and more accurate.


Since the voltages had noticeable discrepancies at corresponding locations between the uniform block and the non-uniform block, we successfully showed a non-destructive testing method using measurements of electric voltage to reveal defects in conductive materials.
